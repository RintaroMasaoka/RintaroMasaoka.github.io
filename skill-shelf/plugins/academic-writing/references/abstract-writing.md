# Abstract Writing

Write an abstract that communicates the work's intellectual content to its
intended reader without requiring the reader to have already understood the
work. Its compression must preserve the connections needed to interpret the
problem, answer, and grounds for the answer. This workflow covers drafting,
substantive revision, and whole-abstract review; preserve review-only scope.

## Recover the content and audience before drafting

Read the manuscript or available source argument. Separate established
results, assumptions, reported external results, and unresolved work. Do not
turn a desired conclusion or a writing plan into a finding. A study note may
explain known results; do not give it a claim of research novelty.

From the source and conversation, recover a brief working account outside the
abstract:

- What question, subject, or contribution organizes the work in its genre?
- What does the work establish, explain, or synthesize, what supports that
  content, and which limits materially affect it?
- What does this audience already know, and what belongs to this particular
  topic rather than to the audience's general disciplinary preparation?
- What must the abstract communicate for this audience to identify the work's
  content, significance, and relevance?

The work's subject and contribution determine what the abstract represents;
the audience's knowledge determines which information and connections must be
made explicit. These are separate decisions. A synthesis of familiar results
can be valuable without creating a new puzzle or teaching unfamiliar material.

Infer a stable audience from evidence. If an unresolved audience distinction
would change the information selected, ask one narrow question. Do not infer
ignorance of a whole field from unfamiliarity with this topic, or infer topic
expertise from competence in the field. Do not print this working account as
prerequisite declarations or teaching objectives inside the abstract.

The intended reader should be able to identify the problem, the substantive
answer or contribution, and enough of its basis and scope to understand what
the work establishes. They need not know the derivation or every named
mechanism. These are acceptance questions, not mandatory sentence slots or a
fixed order. Respect venue-specific abstract formats when supplied.

## Select information before choosing wording

Select propositions and relations, not a quota of technical terms. For each
candidate detail, ask what it contributes to identifying the problem,
understanding the answer, assessing its basis, or delimiting applicability.
Keep the minimum relations that make those contributions intelligible.

A technical name may be essential to identify the object or distinguish the
result. Keep it when it serves that purpose for this audience. A model's formal
name, notation, mechanism label, derivation step, or secondary comparison can
instead belong in the body even when it is scientifically important.
Expanding every unfamiliar term into an explanatory phrase preserves the same
information burden and can make the abstract longer without making it useful.
Conversely, removing names must not remove the specific result and leave only
generic promises to examine, compare, or clarify.

Preserve conditions that change the answer. Compress other detail without
inflating a conditional or externally supported result into an unconditional
claim. If the source does not supply the missing argument, report the gap
outside the abstract rather than disguising it with fluent prose.

## Separate reporting from using a premise

An abstract can report a fact the reader does not yet know. It cannot silently
require the reader to supply the unreported connection that makes the next
claim intelligible. Reporting an unfamiliar result and using it as though its
meaning and relevance were already shared are different operations.

Read the candidate from left to right. At each inference distinguish:

- knowledge supported by the audience baseline;
- information actually communicated earlier in this abstract;
- a new result being reported now;
- a relation understood only from the body, the authoring conversation, or
  the writer's knowledge of the completed argument.

The last category needs a decision: state the necessary connection, defer the
dependent detail, or remove it. Do not automatically add a definition. A phrase
such as "using the dual description" may hide why that description applies
to this object; defining "duality" would not supply the missing connection.
Likewise, saying "it is known" can mark a reported fact but does not explain
an unstated relation required by the following inference.

This is not a demand to prove every claim in the abstract. A new theorem or
reported observation may enlarge the reader's knowledge directly. The test is
whether its objects, role, and connection to the central problem are readable,
not whether the reader can independently derive it.

## Keep the abstract's function

Summarize the argument's content, not the sequence of explanatory tasks used
to produce it. Do not replace an abstract with a staged opening question, a
basic-concept lesson, the first derivation in the body, or a list of what the
author will introduce. A genuine problem can be stated as a question, but
question wording is neither required nor evidence of motivation.

An abstract can start with context, a result, or a problem. Choose the entry
point from the actual audience and argument rather than always adding more
background after feedback about abruptness. Context earns its place by making
the particular problem or result interpretable. First-person contribution
statements and concise methodological descriptions remain valid when they
communicate substantive information rather than intentions alone.

## Draft, cold read, revise

After drafting, assess the text without using the body or conversation to fill
in missing connections. Keep the licensed audience baseline. If using an
independent reader, provide that baseline and the abstract without the author's
rationale or earlier verdicts; do not call a same-context reread independent.

Ask the reader to reconstruct from the abstract alone:

- what particular problem is at stake;
- what the work actually establishes or explains;
- why the stated evidence or mechanism bears on that result;
- what important restriction qualifies the result.

Compare that reconstruction with the source-supported working account. Inspect
the earliest point where comprehension required an unstated premise. Also test
whether the text is merely a plan or whether reducing detail has erased the
answer. Reviewer approval alone is insufficient: inspect what the reviewer
could reconstruct and what they silently supplied.

Revise information selection and connections before polishing sentences, then
repeat the cold read on the revised text. Once the central account is
recoverable at the intended background level, assess remaining improvements
in precision, concision, register, rhythm, and compliance with venue constraints.
Judge each revision by the concrete improvement it makes while preserving the
content and audience fit. Stop when no material defect remains and no further
change has a clear benefit within the requested scope.

If successive variants cannot be evaluated against stable criteria, pause
local rewriting and recover the work's central account, genre, and audience.
Resume when a specific improvement can be identified. Ask only if a
consequential premise remains unresolved. Bound delegated review according to
the hosting workflow; repetition does not authorize unlimited reviewer spawning.

Return clean abstract prose for a writing request. Keep drafting history,
audience ledgers, rejected versions, and review discussion outside it. For a
review request, report concrete failures and repairs rather than rewriting
beyond scope. State any unresolved evidential or audience limitation honestly.

## Boundary checks

- For specialists, a named method can be the shortest sufficient connection;
  do not unpack it just because a novice would not know the name.
- For readers new to an application, knowing the field's methods does not
  establish why a particular method applies in that application.
- A theorem-first abstract may report new objects and results if their role
  and scope are legible. It need not begin with a question or basic definition.
- A proposal abstract can state planned work when the genre calls for a plan;
  it must not present expected findings as completed results.
- Short abstracts can omit secondary mechanisms and comparisons. They must
  retain the conditions needed to avoid misrepresenting the central answer.
