# Source Material — Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) on Video-Making & Exposition

Collected via web research (2026-07-09). Quotes are reported as extracted from the
source; a few are paraphrase-level confidence rather than verbatim and are flagged as
such. Do not upgrade a flagged paraphrase to a quoted line without independently
re-checking the source. Cluster order matches `SKILL.md`.

## 1. SoME (Summer of Math Exposition) judging criteria — most explicit, most primary

Source: 3blue1brown.com/blog/some1/ (primary), 3blue1brown.com/blog/some1-results/ (primary)

- **Clarity**: "Jargon should be explained, the goals of the lesson should be
  understandable with minimal background, and the submission should generally display
  empathy for people unfamiliar with the topic."
- **Motivation**: "It should be clear to the reader/viewer within the first 30 seconds
  why they should care."
- **Novelty**: value comes from "covering common topics in better ways" or "surfacing
  otherwise obscure ideas which more people should know about" — explicitly *not*
  satisfied by nicer visuals on an existing explanation; requires a genuinely different
  angle.
- **Memorability**: "Something should make the piece easy to remember even several
  months later. Maybe it's the beauty of the presentation, the enthusiasm of the
  presenter, or the mind-blowingness of an aha moment."
- Not ranked by popularity/views, because that "punishes more niche content with very
  deliberate and specific target audience."
- Summary line: excellent exposition = "clarity, strong motivation, compelling choices
  of topic matter, and non-obvious insights."

## 2. Concreteness before abstraction / pedagogical order ≠ logical order

- Lex Fridman #64 (podscripts.co transcript): "It will be more compelling and easier
  to understand when you flip that" (definition-then-example → example-then-definition).
  "Each choice, you're showing a concrete example, with each concrete example, you're
  aiding someone's path to understanding."
- 3blue1brown.com/about/ (WebFetch summary, paraphrase-level): "Never Start with
  Definitions: Topic definitions represent an endpoint, not a starting point." "The
  best pedagogical order of ideas is often very different from the correct logical
  order." "Concrete Before Abstract: Present examples before general frameworks,
  opening with key exercises rather than ending with them."
- IAPS interview (iaps.info, Jan 2025): "I applied a visuals-first approach to real
  course material."
- Secondary (moderate confidence, consistent with the above): "resist the temptation
  to open a topic by describing a general result or definition, and instead let
  examples precede generality... finding the type of example which guides the audience
  to rediscover the general results for themselves."

## 3. Earning the payoff / surprise

- Perell podcast: "I think to gain a new understanding of something, it has to be
  information flowing from inside your head to out of it, not the other way around."
  "the actual value add I think lies almost entirely on the inspiration half."
- Secondary (re: inscribed-rectangle video): describes a payoff moment as a "climax,"
  drawing an explicit analogy to "a kiss in a fairytale" — two separately developed
  lines of reasoning finally converging to resolve the problem.
- Lex Fridman #64 (favorite-video discussion, inscribed square problem): "The
  construction here is very abstract in nature... once you see it, it's quite pretty.
  And it's very satisfying to see a specific instance of it." Describes being
  surprised himself mid-production by the visualized surface resembling "a Sydney
  Opera House or some sort of Frank Gehry design."

## 4. Narrative structure

- Secondary (consistent across sources): "Every video starts with a narrative or
  storyline... revolves around imagery that illuminates the beauty of mathematics."
  Discovery-first process: "Rather than scripting videos ahead of time, Sanderson
  makes the playground and then plays around, then sees what stories there are to
  tell within that."
- Stanford Daily (2020, reporting a Stanford talk, closer to verbatim):
  - "When people engage with fiction, no one ever asks, 'When am I going to use
    this?' The same thing fiction can do for people, so can math. There's wonder and
    mystery — and once there's a story, you can go behind the mechanics."
  - "We are not coming from a motivation of usefulness. We're coming from story and
    intrinsic delight. But even when you try not to make math useful, it has a way of
    coming back at you."
  - "When we put the visuals first, then start to articulate the meaning, you can get
    a sense of ownership over it."
  - "I want people to see that whatever your background is, you are not different
    from the top student."
  - "The people at the end who made it through weren't the ones who had more
    intuition. They were the ones who were flexible enough to try different ways of
    learning."
- Dwarkesh interview part 1: "A lot of it is just deciding what's worth saying in the
  first place, what's worth putting there." "People listening trust your curation on
  what's an interesting topic in the first place." Cites Timothy Chow's notion of
  "unsolved expository problems" — results that are proven but not yet *understood* in
  an exposition-ready sense: "There is a difference between proof and explanation."
  Claims clear communicators and people with genuinely novel insight tend to overlap
  (Einstein, Shannon, Feynman) — clarity as evidence of real understanding, not a
  separate skill layered on top.

## 5. Pacing and viewer cognition

- Lex Fridman #118 (happyscribe transcript): most viewers will simply "consume what
  the author had in mind" rather than exploring interactive elements themselves —
  conclusion: deliberate authorial pacing control matters more than offering
  interactive freedom, because most people won't use the freedom anyway.
  On research process: "I'm really going to try to approach this before I see how
  this person went at it... you gain all sorts of articulable intuitions about that
  topic" — reconstructing an idea independently first, so the *route* to the insight
  (not just the insight) survives into the teaching.
- Secondary, well-corroborated but anecdotal: calm delivery and pauses give viewers
  time to map spoken explanation onto changing symbols/images, a deliberate rejection
  of a "firehose of facts" pace. "Pause and ponder" is associated with his content
  style as a recurring segment/idea; unclear whether it is his own coined term or a
  community label — do not quote it as his own phrase without independent
  verification.

## 6. Empathy for the viewer

- Dwarkesh interview part 1: "One of the most valuable things that you can have if
  you're trying to explain stuff online is a sense of empathy for what possible
  viewers that are out there." On empathy decaying with distance from the audience's
  life stage: "The more distance that you put between yourself and them in terms of
  life circumstances... that distance just makes it more and more of an uphill battle
  to make the content good for them" — cites his own growing distance from being a
  student as an example of empathy he has to actively work to recover.
  "It's so in our bones that it's hard to empathize with not having numeracy."
- Dwarkesh interview part 2: good teachers can say "Actually, you're not really
  thinking about this topic the correct way" — diagnosing *how* a student is
  misconceiving something, because "a student's mental structures are not the same as
  the explainer's." Framed as a theory-of-mind requirement distinct from just knowing
  the material.

## 7. What to cut / editorial restraint

- Terence Tao's blog on their cosmic-distance-ladder collaboration
  (terrytao.wordpress.com, Feb 2025) — Tao's own words describing the cut, not
  Grant's: the video "contained some minor inaccuracies and omissions (including some
  made for editing reasons to keep the overall narrative coherent and within a
  reasonable length)." Chose to "focus primarily on the first time each rung of the
  ladder was climbed" over later refinements. Modern context (dark matter, CMB,
  multi-messenger astronomy) was "largely omitted" to preserve one clean historical
  throughline instead of surveying current methods. A full scene (Al-Biruni's
  measurement) was cut "for time constraints, and the need for editing to tighten the
  narrative."
- Secondary (moderate confidence): editorial cuts are described as inseparable from
  his own authoring/thinking process, not a separable post-production step — hard to
  hand off to someone else to execute.

## 8. Process — scripting/storyboarding/research

- Lex Fridman #118: "my job... It's not just to make the uh, the visuals that someone
  will look at. It's to be the one to decide what's the interesting thing to walk
  through here." Prefers writing out and thinking about setting over improvising and
  editing later. On the hardest part of production: "writing... that's the worst."
  Writer's-block fix: "read... and then go back to writing." On what makes a topic
  video-worthy: "Coming up with the magical thing that ties this whole story
  together" — a topic isn't greenlit until the unifying insight/thread is found, not
  merely because the topic is important. Research style: "you're like ambient
  learning a ton of things just in the background and then whence you feel like you
  have the understanding for one, you put it on the list."
- Perell interview: "It looks like reading books or being willing to get caught into
  a rabbit hole of an interesting question, and not feeling guilty about part of the
  day washing away to that effect."
- IAPS interview: "I start with whatever sparks my own interest and curiosity." "If
  I'm not genuinely fascinated by a topic, I doubt I could convey it in a way that
  excites others." "What matters most isn't always what gets the highest views."
- Perell interview, on relative difficulty: problem-solving videos are comparatively
  easy to script — "you know the end point and you know the start point, so you just
  have to draw a verbal line between the two." Expository videos are harder because
  the job is finding "perspectives on things that are known, rather than trying to
  forge into things that are unknown" — the difficulty shifts from finding the answer
  to finding the angle.
- Dwarkesh transcript (WebFetch summary, paraphrase-level confidence): "I can't put
  into words what I want except through code. That's my thinking mode" — for him the
  animation/code layer is part of working out the idea, not illustration bolted onto
  a finished script.

## 9. Rigor, jargon, what not to include

- Lex Fridman #118: critiques topology popularization that fixates on the
  coffee-mug/donut homeomorphism trivia instead of explaining why mathematicians
  actually care about general structures — a vivid analogy that doesn't connect to
  why the field's practitioners care is a named failure mode. On avoiding both
  extremes when teaching topology: "either it's too squishy... or it's a little bit
  too rigor first." On exponential growth specifically: real understanding requires
  moving past "observing patterns" to understanding *mechanism* (the underlying
  proportional relationship), not just recognizing the shape of a curve.
- SoME clarity criterion (repeated from §1): jargon isn't banned, but must not be
  assumed as background — it must be explained.

## 10. Learning philosophy for the audience (retention, exercises)

- Lex Fridman #64: "Try to force yourself to do more problems than you naturally
  would." Recommends looking at chapter-end exercises before reading the chapter.
- Perell interview: "unless you do something active, like reinventing it yourself,
  like doing problems to solidify it... it's not actually going to stick." Satisfying
  delivery "does not correlate with long-term learning" — a direct, self-aware
  critique of his own medium: video provides motivation/intuition/inspiration: "the
  actual value add I think lies almost entirely on the inspiration half"; the mastery
  that sticks requires the viewer's own active work afterward.
- Dwarkesh transcript (paraphrase-level): "Skipping calculations shoots yourself in
  the foot. Working through them solidifies intuitions." "Without homework's forcing
  function, people skip the work and it doesn't sink in as well."

## 11. Visual semantics / motion — a genuine gap

No primary-source Grant Sanderson quote was found stating an explicit rule like
"color must always carry meaning" or a fixed color-coding convention across videos.
Closest material:

- 3blue1brown.com/about/ (WebFetch summary, paraphrase-level): "Avoid Unnecessary
  Animation: Every movement on the screen should be deliberate, with an identifiable
  purpose."
- Lex Fridman #118: "I'm not entirely sold that the movement matters that much for
  the intuitions," while separately arguing visuals matter because they "opened up
  the scope of what people can have intuitions for" by leveraging visual processing
  capacity — i.e. he is not fully convinced *motion itself* (vs. the underlying static
  visual/geometric representation) is what does the pedagogical work.
- Config 2026 talk "Designing Math ft. Grant Sanderson" (YouTube, not transcribed) —
  described by search results as covering visual design, storytelling, and workflow,
  framing math communication as a design problem ("where there is choice, there is
  design"). Worth watching directly if a verbatim color/motion-semantics quote is
  ever needed; not extracted here.

If the skill needs a stronger color-as-semantics rule than "avoid unnecessary
animation," source it from Manim community convention or direct observation of the
videos, not from an attributed Grant Sanderson quote — none was found.
