# Working-memory review for mathematical notes

Apply this to the visible reading path, with hidden details closed. This is an editorial test of what the text asks a reader to keep available, not a numerical claim about human memory capacity. Inspect an argument forward, without using later paragraphs to rescue an earlier transition. Keep an internal dependency ledger: current goal, objects and notation needed now, established results still in use, and temporary calculations. For each later use, identify the earlier statement it actually depends on.

## Three time scales

1. **Within a formula or paragraph.** List symbols that must be distinguished simultaneously. Detect overloaded letters, visually similar symbols, remote definitions, and notation whose name gives no cue to its role. Reduce or rename symbols, group recurring data into a named object, or restate the local meaning beside the formula. Merely defining every symbol somewhere is insufficient.

   Treat a newly introduced Latin or Greek base letter as a substantial memory cost. This differs from generating members of one already understood family by indices, such as $x_i$: the family supplies a shared semantic cue, though its indices still need review. Prefer a letter whose established mathematical or mnemonic association matches its role, such as $m$ for mass. A matching association compresses the definition; an arbitrary letter adds a separate letter-to-object mapping that the reader must keep active.

   Distinguish total notation from simultaneous introduction. A symbol that has appeared repeatedly throughout the required reading path, with a stable meaning, may have become one familiar chunk; that can make a later new symbol affordable. Never use this to justify a burst of notation: introducing three or four new base letters at one transition is a failure regardless of whether each is immediately defined. Stage the construction so that no more than one or two new base letters enter before the reader can use and stabilize them.

   If a new symbol is genuinely unavoidable, attach its semantic noun phrase every time it appears while it is part of the new load: write “the mass $m$,” “the covariance $P$,” or an equally specific phrase, rather than a bare letter. Do not assume that one earlier definition remains active. For a display in which the symbol cannot naturally carry prose, put the semantic phrase or a compact legend immediately before or after that display, in the same reading unit. If repeated labels make the passage intolerable, that is evidence that the notation should be reduced, packaged, or introduced later. A symbol may lose this mandatory epithet only after repeated use has made its meaning stable across the required reading path; the review must identify evidence of that familiarity, not merely elapsed pages.

   As a default, introduce at most one or two new base letters that lack an immediately usable conventional or mnemonic association. This is not permission to introduce two when prose or an existing named object would be clearer. If more are needed, reuse established notation, name and package the data as one object, split the argument, or delay each symbol until its first real use. Do not rename standard source notation merely to create a mnemonic if the renaming would make comparison harder; instead give a short source-to-note bridge and keep the scope explicit.
2. **Across an argument or section.** Before a computation, make clear which question it answers. After a coherent step, package its output in a form the next step can use. Then inspect every later use: a boundary releases the temporary chain only if later steps depend on the packaged output rather than the expansion, case split, or auxiliary variable. If a detail remains necessary, carry it explicitly, rederive it locally, or reorganize the argument. Split a long chain or prove a lemma when that changes the dependencies; a recap alone does not.
3. **Across sections and chapters.** At the point of reuse, restate the smallest earlier result and its relevant assumptions, or link to a stable reference. Reintroduce notation that has not been active. Do not require recall of the earlier derivation or of a chapter-opening roadmap. Verify that the new argument depends on this result and these assumptions; if it still uses an earlier calculation detail, restore that detail where it is used.

## Review procedure

- Mark each point where the goal, object, notation, or argument stage changes. For each point ask: *What is being established now? Which exact earlier statements do the next steps use? Has a derived result replaced its intermediate steps in every downstream use?* Do not infer a release from a transition sentence alone.
- At every new base-letter introduction, ask: *What prior association will help the reader recover its meaning? Is that association compatible with its role here? How many arbitrary base-letter mappings are already live? Where is the semantic epithet attached at every occurrence?* Count Latin and Greek letters alike. Do not excuse an arbitrary symbol merely because it was defined in the preceding sentence. Treat three or more simultaneously introduced base letters as a structural defect to repair, not a wording issue.
- If the answer requires holding an expanding list of intermediate results, revise the structure. Possible repairs: compress several lines into one named result, introduce a short lemma, separate independent cases, repeat the needed formula locally, move optional algebra behind a disclosure, or replace symbols with names that cue their roles. Keep premises and the result itself in the visible path.
- After revision, read the path again with details closed. A transition passes when its purpose and inputs can be recovered from nearby text, and the subsequent derivation actually uses the compressed result. Check every later use: if it invokes an intermediate detail directly, the claimed release failed and the dependency must be repaired.

For example, after a Wick expansion has produced a cumulant identity, a later calculation can use that identity and its parameters without carrying the individual pairings. If a later coefficient depends on one particular pairing, the dependency is not cut: show that pairing again at the point of use, or change the argument so that the identity suffices.

Do not add “forget this” instructions, mechanical release labels, or summary sentences that merely claim the dependency ended. The published mathematics should demonstrate the release: downstream formulas use the packaged result, temporary notation disappears, and any needed detail is recovered locally.
