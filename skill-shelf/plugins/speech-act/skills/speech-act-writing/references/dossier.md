## Conceptual references

### austin1962 — J. L. Austin, How to Do Things with Words (1962)

The dossier supports considering an utterance in its circumstances, including written acts, and distinguishing illocutionary action from later effects. Understanding meaning and force belongs to uptake; compliance is a different matter. The p. 116 passage distinguishes conventional taking effect, uptake, and later responses.

Use in O1, O2, O3, O4, and O5: separate intended act, recognized act, and consequence; check relevant circumstances. Do not infer that recognizable wording alone fulfills every condition.

Access reported: institutional excerpts identified as pp. 5–8 and 139; indexed scan passages at pp. 109 and 116; a 1975 edition publisher abstract and record. Direct scan retrieval failed. The institutional excerpt's edition provenance is unresolved.

References: [institutional excerpts](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ihum54/Austin_on_speech_acts.htm), [primary scan](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2271128_6/component/file_2271430/austin_1962_how-to-do-things-with-words.pdf), [1975 edition record](https://academic.oup.com/book/5162).

### searle1965 — John R. Searle, What Is a Speech Act? (1965)

The dossier distinguishes constitutive rules from rules regulating an independently existing activity. It reports a promising analysis focused on explicit central cases, with borderline, defective, hinted, metaphorical, and elliptical cases outside its selected scope. An institutional excerpt includes meaningful written marks.

Use in O1: ask which supplied conditions make an act count in a setting, separately from preferences about its execution. This is not a complete institutional-validity test or a reconstruction of promise conditions.

Access reported: indexed reprint passages at pp. 2, 3, and 10 and an edited institutional excerpt; direct PDFs were not successfully retrieved.

References: [institutional reprint](https://jomi.das.ufsc.br/mas/leituras/SpeechActs-Searle.pdf), [Open University excerpt](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/ocw/pluginfile.php/610540/mod_resource/content/1/reading2.pdf).

### searle1976 — John R. Searle, A classification of illocutionary acts (1976)

The abstract proposes representatives/assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations. It names point, direction of fit, and expressed psychological state among the distinguishing dimensions. It presents a theoretical classification with criteria rather than a grammar-based inventory.

Use in O2: distinguish what an act does from sentence form and allow classifications to remain tentative. The skill's ordinary working labels are design choices. Detailed definitions, category mappings, and diagnostic tests are not supplied by this abstract.

Access reported: publisher abstract, issue metadata, and references only. The 2008 online date is distinct from the 1976 publication.

Reference: [publisher record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-in-society/article/abs/classification-of-illocutionary-acts1/C51AB2FEB1BFD115FE9B066A8669789D).

### grice1975 — H. P. Grice, Logic and Conversation (1975)

The inspected passages distinguish what is said from conventional and conversational implicatures, while acknowledging context in reference and disambiguation. They describe exchanges with a common purpose or accepted direction and expectations of quantity, quality, relation, and manner.

Use in O2 and O3: expose consequential implications and ask whether needed information and evidential qualifications are explicit enough for the task. These expectations are not a compulsory prose checklist, and every reader inference need not be intended.

Access reported: indexed primary passages; the opened scan yielded no extractable text and screenshots were unusable. No complete essay reading was claimed.

Reference: [institutional scan](https://web.stanford.edu/class/psych205/papers/Grice-1975.pdf).

### stalnaker1998 — Robert Stalnaker, On the Representation of Context (1998)

The abstract describes context as information presumed shared and presupposed when acts are interpreted. Acts depend on context and also affect it. Presumed sharing is not evidence that participants actually believe the same things.

Use in O2 and O5: identify what readers are being treated as already knowing, and track changes to those assumptions across an exchange. Do not reconstruct the uninspected formal apparatus.

Access reported: publisher abstract and bibliographic record only.

Reference: [publisher record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008254815298).

### clark_brennan1991 — Herbert H. Clark and Susan E. Brennan, Grounding in Communication (1991)

Grounding concerns jointly established understanding sufficient for current purposes. A contribution involves presentation and acceptance supported by evidence of understanding; acceptance does not mean agreement. Email and letters permit review and private revision while changing timing, feedback, sequencing, repair, and coordination costs.

Use in O5: distinguish issuing a reply from establishing understanding, and design a repair opportunity suited to the exchange. The chapter sets one-way broadcast media aside. Applying its questions to an article without recipient evidence is an analogy, not proof of grounding.

Access reported: selected passages at pp. 127–132 and 140–146, not an exhaustive chapter reading.

Reference: [author-hosted chapter](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Clark%2C%20H.H.%20_%20Brennan%2C%20S.E.%20_Grounding%20in%20communication_%201991.pdf).

### wilson_sperber2004 — Deirdre Wilson and Dan Sperber, Relevance Theory (2004)

Decoding is one input to inferential interpretation. Context contributes to explicit content as well as implicatures. Relevance concerns comparative cognitive effects and processing effort. Recognizing an informative intention is different from believing what is communicated. The account disputes parts of Grice's explanation.

Use in O2 and O4: expose underdetermination, distinguish understanding from belief, and consider context without treating relevance as a numerical score. Do not assume intention is transparently recoverable.

Access reported: selected author-hosted sections, including §§1–3 and explicit-content discussion; no full chapter reading was claimed.

Reference: [author-hosted chapter](https://www.dan.sperber.fr/?p=93).

### miller1984 — Carolyn R. Miller, Genre as Social Action (1984)

The abstract locates genre in recurrent rhetorical action and conventionalized social motives, rather than merely subject matter or form.

Use in O1: connect genre selection to the action and recurrent situation, rather than choosing a visual template alone. Do not infer detailed arguments about exigence, typification, or levels of meaning from the abstract.

Access reported: author-uploaded abstract and bibliographic records only. Later upload and display dates do not replace the original 1984 publication year.

References: [author-uploaded record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238749675_Genre_as_Social_Action), [bibliographic record](https://genreacrossborders.uwaterloo.ca/biblio/genre-social-action-0).

### bazerman2004 — Charles Bazerman, Speech Acts, Genres, and Activity Systems: How Texts Organize Activity and People (2004)

Texts participate in relationships, social facts, and coordinated activities. A genre set concerns a role's text types; a genre system concerns patterned relations among interacting participants' sets; an activity system includes what people accomplish through and beyond texts. Extended writing can aggregate acts under dominant purposes. Responses can be delayed, private, multiple, or differently situated. Genre recognition is not exhausted by a fixed feature list.

Use in O1, O2, O4, and O5: analyze local passages within larger texts and document sequences, and avoid assuming a single immediate response. Identify the author's broader term perlocutionary uptake separately from Austin's uptake.

Access reported: conceptual and initial methodological sections at pp. 309–320; later analyses and exercises were not comprehensively inspected.

Reference: [author-hosted chapter](https://bazerman.education.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/docs/Bazerman2004CHAPSpeechActsGenresActivitySystems.pdf).

### hyland2005 — Ken Hyland, Stance and engagement: a model of interaction in academic discourse (2005)

The abstract distinguishes writer positioning toward material from resources that presuppose or construct an active addressee. The account draws on 240 published research articles across eight disciplines and informant interviews, supporting attention to disciplinary writer-reader positioning.

Use in O1, O2, O3, and O4: describe what position and reader relationship a passage constructs. The dossier does not supply the full linguistic feature taxonomy or causal evidence of particular readers' reactions.

Access reported: institutional abstract, metadata, and author publication-list entry only.

Reference: [institutional record](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/stance-and-engagement-a-model-of-interaction-in-academic-discours/).

## AI-mediated communication references

### hohenstein2023 — Hohenstein et al., Artificial intelligence in communication impacts language and social relationships (2023)

Two randomized messenger experiments concerned short policy discussions. Increased smart-reply use was associated with faster exchanges, more positive emotional language, and favorable partner evaluations. Study 1 instrumented use with randomized availability; causal interpretation depends on instrumental-variable assumptions. Suspected AI use was associated with less favorable impressions, but suspicion was not randomized. Availability alone did not yield the same main effects as use.

Use in O3 and O4: keep changes in expression separate from beliefs about production and interpersonal reception. Do not convert the suspicion association into a causal disclosure penalty or a universal authenticity claim.

Access reported: abstract, selected introduction/discussion passages, and selected methods sections.

Reference: [article](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-30938-9).

### purcell2025 — Purcell et al., Writing with AI boosts trust-building efficiency (2025)

Two preregistered online incentivized trust-game experiments, total N=1,637, found reduced writing time with generally similar behavioral and stated trust. Disclosure did not substantially remove the efficiency advantage. Study 2 found a small behavioral-trust advantage for unaided messages without a corresponding stated-trust effect. Linguistic associations with warmth, complexity, and lower authenticity were exploratory; historical familiarity was a proposed explanation, not a tested change over time.

Use in O3 and O4: distinguish behavioral trust, stated trust, linguistic associations, and proposed mechanisms. Do not generalize from brief US transactions to scholarly acceptance, emotional closeness, or long-term cooperation.

Access reported: summary, selected Study 2 results and figure descriptions, discussion/limitations/conclusions, and selected STAR Methods.

Reference: [article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.114092).

### jakesch2023 — Maurice Jakesch, Jeffrey T. Hancock, and Mor Naaman, Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed (2023)

Six experiments, N=4,600, tested source judgments for professional, dating, and hospitality self-presentations generated with GPT-2/GPT-3-era models. Judgments were near chance. First-person language and family references predicted perceived humanity without reliably identifying human origin. Validation using learned judgment predictions increased perceived humanity of selected generated profiles. Immediate feedback and incentives did not improve detection under tested conditions; some detectable differences remained.

Use in O4: keep production attribution separate from interpreting a text and do not apply stylistic detection heuristics as an evaluation shortcut. These results do not establish universal undetectability or rule out learning through longer exposure.

Access reported: selected manuscript results, discussion, and methods, pp. 2–8.

Reference: [article](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208839120).

### hu2023 — Jennifer Hu, Sammy Floyd, Olessia Jouravlev, Evelina Fedorenko, and Edward Gibson, A fine-grained comparison of pragmatic language understanding in humans and language models (2023)

Expert-curated English multiple-choice scenarios tested seven pragmatic phenomena. Larger tested models often selected intended interpretations and shared human error patterns, while humor, irony, and maxim violations remained difficult. Removing context degraded human and model performance, especially for irony, deceits, and maxims. Models retained substantial performance on scrambled contexts; comparable human scrambling tests were not conducted.

Use in O2 and O4: retain relevant context and label model-generated interpretations as task-limited proposals. Linguistic cue learning and heuristic mental-state representation remained competing explanations. Answer selection does not establish consciousness, the model's own intentions, or reliable access to a particular user's unstated goals.

Access reported: selected §§3–6, pp. 4195–4202.

Reference: [conference paper](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.230/).

### pennanen2026 — Niki Pennanen, Pyry Kanerva, and Christian Guckelsberger, How prompting and editing shape psychological ownership in AI-assisted creative writing (2026)

An abstract-level account reports a preregistered short-story experiment, N=273, crossing single versus iterative prompting with direct-editing availability and including manual writing. Editing increased ownership, especially after single-prompt generation. Iterative prompting increased ownership when editing was unavailable. Manual writing yielded higher ownership than all AI-assisted conditions. Prompting and editing as partially substitutable opportunities for control and self-investment is the authors' interpretation.

Use in O3 and O4: offer meaningful control opportunities and distinguish the writer's reported ownership from other outcomes. Transferring this rationale to scholarly revision is a design inference. Legal authorship, communicative intention, actual contribution, and reader reception were not interchangeable with the measured ownership outcome.

Access reported: publisher highlights/abstract and institutional metadata/abstract only. Full methods and measurement details were unavailable.

Reference: [article record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2026.100381).

### google-smart-compose — Google, Use Smart Compose in Gmail (undated documentation)

The documentation describes suggestions accepted through an explicit action, separate suggestion and personalization controls, generic suggestions when personalization is off, and personalization aimed at matching usual writing style. It warns that suggestions may predict incorrect facts and are not designed to answer questions.

Use in O3: distinguish a proposed wording, its selection, and fidelity to a particular communicative purpose. The interface provides an analogy for visible selection, not empirical proof of preserved intent, ownership, or agency. The skill does not require this product or imitate its interface.

Access reported: specified opening notices and controls/personalization sections, inspected in the supplied dossier on 6 October 2026.

Reference: [first-party documentation](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/9116836?co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop&hl=en).

## Cross-source limits

Hohenstein and Purcell studied different assistance, tasks, expectations, and outcomes. Their findings neither directly replicate one another nor establish that society adapted between publication dates. Immediate detection-feedback failures do not resolve durable reader adaptation. None of these sources validates the skill's workflow, a universal academic style, or a mandatory disclosure practice.

The conceptual works supply distinctions rather than one settled theory. In particular, presumed context, grounding acceptance, proposition belief, request compliance, institutional taking effect, and experienced ownership must remain distinguishable. Use the operations to make these questions explicit, not to certify answers that the supplied evidence leaves open.
