# Source map

Research date: 2026-10-06. The research prioritizes original texts, author and publisher records, and practitioners' own guides. Each entry states the scope actually checked. Inspecting original passages extracted by search is not equivalent to reading the whole text or visually inspecting every PDF page.

**Findings from the literature** and **writing methods synthesized by this package** are distinct. [Theory and operating principles](foundations.md) and [Practice in the AI era](ai-practice.md) explicitly label the latter. The package distributes original summaries and practical synthesis with source links, rather than long quotations or copies of third-party works.

## Theory, pragmatics, and academic writing

### F01 — Austin: content, act, and effect

J. L. Austin (1962), *How to Do Things with Words*. [Original scan](https://archive.org/download/austin-how-to-do-things-with-words/Austin%2C%20how%20to%20do%20things%20with%20words_text.pdf), [OCR](https://archive.org/stream/austin-how-to-do-things-with-words/Austin%2C%20how%20to%20do%20things%20with%20words_djvu.txt). The original is held by a third party.

Checked: Lecture II, pp.14–18, and VIII–X, especially pp.116–120. Distinguishes appropriate circumstances and procedures, locution/illocution/perlocution, and uptake. For writing, separate content, force, and hoped-for response. Do not literally transfer the conditions for institutional declarations to ordinary prose or equate uptake with being convinced.

### F02 — Searle: proposition, force, and classes of acts

John R. Searle (1965), "What Is a Speech Act?", in *Philosophy in America*, pp.221–239. [University-hosted reprint](https://jomi.das.ufsc.br/mas/leituras/SpeechActs-Searle.pdf). Searle (1975), "A Taxonomy of Illocutionary Acts", pp.344–369. [University-hosted chapter](https://conservancy.umn.edu/bitstream/11299/185220/1/7-08_Searle.pdf). Related republication (1976), "A Classification of Illocutionary Acts", [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0047404500006837).

Checked: the 1965 introduction, content/force distinction, and conditions; search-extracted original passages from the 1975 opening, classification criteria, and representatives/directives; the 1976 abstract. Original PDFs that could not be retrieved directly were not read in full. Use the distinctions to avoid evaluating assertions, requests, and promises by identical criteria. Rhetorical moves in a text do not map one-to-one onto the five classes, and classification does not establish an LLM's internal psychology.

### F03 — Grice: the current purpose and implication

H. P. Grice (1975), "Logic and Conversation", *Syntax and Semantics 3*, pp.41–58. [University-hosted original](https://web.stanford.edu/class/psych205/papers/Grice-1975.pdf).

Checked: search-extracted original passages on the principle and Quantity, pp.45–46, and examples exploiting maxims, pp.52–53. Not every page of the scan without OCR was visually inspected. Provides a framework for shared purpose, stage, and implication. Do not turn maxims into universal etiquette or a minimum-character-count rule.

### F04 — Stalnaker: assertion and shared assumptions

Robert Stalnaker, "On the Representation of Context" (SALT 1996, journal 1998, book reprint 1999). [SALT record](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/SALT/article/view/2768), [Reprint scan](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/f09/semprag1/stalnaker-context.pdf), [Journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008254815298).

Checked: bibliographic information, abstract, and search-extracted passages from reprint pp.98–103, especially recognizing assertions and accepting or rejecting them. Consider assertion as a proposal to update a background presumed shared. Common ground is neither the actual knowledge of every reader nor a set of truths. This reading does not substitute for having read the original "Assertion" (1978).

### F05 — Clark & Brennan: establishing understanding and the medium

Herbert H. Clark & Susan E. Brennan (1991), "Grounding in Communication", pp.127–149. [Original on the author's site](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Clark%2C%20H.H.%20_%20Brennan%2C%20S.E.%20_Grounding%20in%20communication_%201991.pdf).

Checked: pp.127–135, 140–145, and 148–149. Participants collaboratively establish understanding adequate for the current purpose, considering costs associated with the medium. Paraphrase, application, and subsequent action can provide evidence of understanding. Speculation about a reader model does not observe successful grounding, and understanding differs from agreement.

### F06 — Wilson & Sperber: relevance, inference, and effort

Deirdre Wilson & Dan Sperber (2004), "Relevance Theory", *The Handbook of Pragmatics*, pp.607–632. [Full text posted by the author](https://www.dan.sperber.fr/?p=93).

Checked: §§1–4, comparisons of effort/effect, and qualifications. Interpreting utterances involves inference from background and evidence of intentions. Reduce unnecessary search and consider what the reader gains for judgment. Do not reduce relevance to a numerical ratio or "shorter is better."

### F07 — Miller: genre as social action

Carolyn R. Miller (1984), "Genre as Social Action", *Quarterly Journal of Speech* 70(2), 151–167. [Scan posted by the author](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Carolyn-Miller-15/publication/238749675_Genre_as_Social_Action/links/56bc9c9c08ae6cc737c5c405/Genre-as-Social-Action.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00335638409383686).

Checked: pp.151–153, 156–158, and 163–165. Considers genres as actions responding to recurrent social situations. A document's adequacy cannot be judged by resemblance of outward form alone. Genres depend on communities and histories; the writer's private intention alone does not determine success.

### F08 — Swales: communicative purposes

John M. Swales (1990), *Genre Analysis: English in Academic and Research Settings*. [Publisher's bibliographic record](https://www.cambridgebookshop.co.uk/products/genre-analysis), [Original excerpt](https://solearabiantree.net/namingofparts/pdf/swalesgenreanalysis.pdf).

Checked: original pp.44–49 held by a third party, title page, and copyright. Addresses purposes, environments of production and reception, and the difference between the order of carrying out work and explaining or justifying it. The original CARS diagram and chapter text were not inspected. This does not substantiate a fixed template across disciplines.

### F09 — Gopen & Swan: reader expectations in scientific prose

George D. Gopen & Judith A. Swan (1990), "The Science of Scientific Writing", *American Scientist* 78(6), 550–558. [Authorized reprint](https://courses.ems.psu.edu/styleforstudents/print/c10_p6.html), [Authorized retyped PDF](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~macleod/writing/sciwriting-gopen-swan.pdf).

Checked: revision examples, topic/stress position, old/new information, seven principles, and qualifications. Writing practice concerning how English structures convey connections, topics, and emphasis. The authors also reject mechanical rules. This is neither an experiment estimating effect sizes nor a universal word-order law for every language.

### F10 — Cowan: boundary conditions on memory capacity

Nelson Cowan (2001), "The Magical Number 4 in Short-Term Memory: A Reconsideration of Mental Storage Capacity", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(1), 87–114. [PDF at the author's university](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf).

Checked: abstract, §§1–2, and some published objections. Addresses capacity under controlled conditions, chunking, and boundary conditions. Use it to inspect the burden of retaining unresolved references or new symbols simultaneously. It does not yield a maximum number of paragraphs or bullet points in a paper.

### F11 — Bakhtin: prior utterances and response

M. M. Bakhtin (1986 English translation), "The Problem of Speech Genres", *Speech Genres and Other Late Essays*, pp.60–102. [Original scan](https://criticaltheoryconsortium.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Bakhtin-Speech-Genres.pdf). Draft 1952–1953; translation by Vern W. McGee.

Checked: pp.60–63, 68–70, and 95–97. Considers utterances in relation to prior utterances, addressees, and anticipated responses. Also consider readers' objections, reuse, and delayed responses. This does not warrant inserting imaginary dialogue in every paragraph or guaranteeing psychological effects.

### F12 — Bazerman: how texts organize activities

Charles Bazerman (2004), "Speech Acts, Genres, and Activity Systems: How Texts Organize Activity and People", in *What Writing Does and How It Does It*, pp.309–339. [PDF at the author's university](https://bazerman.education.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/docs/Bazerman2004CHAPSpeechActsGenresActivitySystems.pdf).

Checked: the opening example of a chain of documents and definitions of speech acts and felicity conditions. Addresses how texts operate through connections with other documents, institutions, and activities. Design a paper as a document participating in evaluation and reuse. Do not confuse the establishment of social facts with the establishment of physical truth.

### F13 — Hyland: stance and engagement

Ken Hyland (2005), "Stance and engagement: a model of interaction in academic discourse", *Discourse Studies* 7(2), 173–192. [University record and abstract](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/stance-and-engagement-a-model-of-interaction-in-academic-discours/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461445605050365).

Checked: bibliographic information and abstract only. An analytical framework for stance and reader engagement, based on 240 papers from eight disciplines and interviews. Refer to it when considering interpersonal functions of scholarly writing. Do not assert details of individual linguistic features or causal effects without reading the full text.

### F14 — Rochester: teaching introductions as moves

Katherine Schaefer, "CARS Intro", University of Rochester Writing, Speaking, and Argument Program. [Practitioner's introduction to the handout](https://writing.rochester.edu/faculty/wsap/resources/cars-intro/). Publication date unknown; checked 2026-10-06.

Checked: the introduction page. A teaching practice analyzing the functions of introductions as moves/steps based on Swales. It is neither a substitute for original research nor proof of effectiveness in every discipline. The linked handout itself was not inspected.

## AI co-writing and current practice

### A01 — Dippold: audience design and relational expectations

Doris Dippold (2024), "Making the Case for Audience Design in Conversational AI: Users’ Pragmatic Strategies and Rapport Expectations in Interaction with a Task-Oriented Chatbot", *Applied Linguistics*. [Publication record](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Making-the-Case-for-Audience-Design/99882866102346), [Earlier manuscript](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.10694).

Checked: published abstract; theory, data, conversation examples, and discussion in the earlier manuscript. Do not treat the two versions as identical. Efficiency and relational expectations coexisted in a simulated booking task, with mismatches causing trouble. A qualitative, bounded sample does not support predicting a preference for directness from nationality.

### A02 — Google: conversational cooperation, context, and repair

Google for Developers, Conversation Design, [Learn about conversation](https://developers.google.com/assistant/conversation-design/learn-about-conversation), [Errors](https://developers.google.com/assistant/conversation-design/errors). Pages updated 2024-09-18.

Checked: cooperation, implication, context, turn-taking, and error repair in both documents. Practice in reusing information from the recipient and adapting repair to context. These documents concern the voice platform for discontinued Conversational Actions; question/retry limits and similar details are product-specific. Do not impose them as general rules of writing.

### A03 — Mailchimp: voice and tone

Mailchimp Content Style Guide, [Voice and Tone](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/). Publication date unknown; checked 2026-10-06.

Checked: page text. Practice distinguishing continuity of voice from situational tone, selecting clarity and humor appropriate to readers. A company's brand guide is not academic evidence universally recommending casual language, positive expressions, or jokes.

### A04 — Doshi & Hauser: individual ratings and collective diversity

Anil R. Doshi & Oliver P. Hauser (2024-07-12), "Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content", *Science Advances* 10, eadn5290. [Published version](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290), [Authors' preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.00506).

Checked: abstract, experiment, methods, and discussion in the preprint; search-extracted methods/limitations from the published version. Direct retrieval of the published version failed. A short-story task using GPT-4 ideas found both higher individual ratings and greater similarity across works. These are conditions of short creative writing in English, not evidence of long-term loss of creativity.

### A05 — Jakesch et al.: effects of writing assistance on attitudes

Maurice Jakesch et al. (CHI 2023), "Co-Writing with Opinionated Language Models Affects Users’ Views". [Original HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.00560), [Authors' PDF](https://www.jakesch-lab.org/assets/pdf/aimc_influence.pdf).

Checked: abstract, experiment, attitude results, discussion, and limitations. GPT-3 assistance configured with opinions shifted writing on a particular topic and subsequent self-reported attitudes. Supports distinguishing insertion of a position from revision of expression. Do not generalize to every ordinary revision or lasting attitude change.

### A06 — Hwang et al.: authenticity and agency

Angel Hsing-Chi Hwang et al. (2025), "It was 80% me, 20% AI": Seeking Authenticity in Co-Writing with Large Language Models, *PACM HCI* 9(2), CSCW122. [Final author PDF](https://angelhwang.github.io/doc/CSCW_LLM_authenticity.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3711020).

Checked: abstract, methods, findings, discussion, and limitations in the final version. Qualitative research including 19 professional writers and 30 readers. Authenticity involved experience, agency, and production processes as well as output style. A small-scale, short-task study does not establish that personalization guarantees voice.

### A07 — Sekizawa & Yanaka: Japanese honorifics and context

Ryo Sekizawa & Hitomi Yanaka (2024), "Do Large Language Models Understand Japanese Honorifics Based on Contextual Information?", *Journal of Natural Language Processing* 31(3), 1292–1329. [Paper](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jnlp/31/3/31_1292/_article/-char/en), [PDF](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jnlp/31/3/31_1292/_pdf/-char/en).

Checked: abstract, dataset, §5.2.3, Table 10, §5.3, and annotator instructions. Addresses conversion failures in complex syntax and differences among native speakers' judgments. Preserve relations among people and quotations, and preserve meaning. Correct honorifics are neither a complete measure of interpersonal appropriateness nor everyone's stylistic preference in Japanese.

### A08 — Hohenstein et al.: emotional expression and interpersonal evaluations

Jess Hohenstein et al. (2023), "Artificial intelligence in communication impacts language and social relationships", *Scientific Reports* 13, 5487. [Published version](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-30938-9).

Checked: abstract, methods of the two experiments, results of the first experiment, and discussion. Smart replies can change emotional tone and interpersonal evaluations. Some findings about perceived AI use are correlational. A publisher correction dated 2023-10-03 was identified but not examined closely; avoid reusing numerical estimates. The paper does not support generalizing to long-form LLM writing, Japanese, or long-term relationships, or recommending concealment of AI use.

### A09 — Sharma et al.: observing sycophancy

Mrinank Sharma et al. (ICLR 2024), "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models". Initial arXiv version 2023; checked v4 (2025-05-10). [Original text](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13548).

Checked: abstract, §§3–4, discussion, and conclusion. Investigates preference data and failures in which five assistants of that period shifted evaluations or answers toward users' preferences and related cues. Distinguish acknowledgment and adaptation from agreement about facts or evaluations. It neither claims that every adaptation is sycophancy nor tests a particular repair prompt's effectiveness.

### A10 — Salvi et al.: persuasion and the 2026 correction

Francesco Salvi et al. (2025-05-19), "On the conversational persuasiveness of GPT-4", *Nature Human Behaviour*. [Published version](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02194-6), [Institutional PDF](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/ccade18d-35d8-45fe-ab98-7ed328cb8816/content), [Author Correction (2026-09-03)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02588-0).

Checked: abstract, randomized debate design, methods, sample, limitations, and the full correction. In short debates with 900 US participants, personalized GPT-4 outperformed the human baseline condition. However, after correction the direct comparison between personalized and nonpersonalized GPT-4 was not significant (P=0.0678, reported as 0.07). Do not state that the additional effect of personalization was established. This measures short-term agreement changes, not authors' intentions or persuasiveness in every situation.

### A11 — Amershi et al.: AI design that supports human correction

Saleema Amershi et al. (CHI 2019), "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction". [Final PDF from Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Guidelines-for-Human-AI-Interaction-camera-ready.pdf).

Checked: abstract, table of 18 guidelines, evaluation method, social norms/bias, and limitations. Addresses correction, dismissal, context, and scope under uncertainty, among other topics. Evaluates the guidelines' applicability; it is not an experiment establishing that each rule causally improves current LLM writing.

### A12 — Martijn et al.: repairing specific trouble

Gabriëlla Martijn et al. (2026), "‘Hold on, I’ll connect you to a human agent’: Recipient design, repair, and their impact on progressivity in human-chatbot conversations", *Discourse & Communication* 20(1), 74–97. [Published version](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17504813261418360), [Institutional record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/hold-on-ill-connect-you-to-a-human-agent-recipient-design-repair-/).

Checked: abstract, data/analysis, repair examples, discussion, and limitations. An observational study sampled 200 Dutch-language conversations and analyzed 132 with handovers (70 finance, 62 public transport). Compared generic bot repairs with human agents' specific questions. Supports retaining understood parts while narrowing uncertainty. This concerns intent-based bots, not a controlled comparison with LLMs.

### A13 — Padmakumar & He: diversity across models

Vishakh Padmakumar & He He (ICLR 2024), "Does Writing with Language Models Reduce Content Diversity?". [Venue](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/02dec8877fb7c6aa9a79f81661baca7c-Abstract-Conference.html), [Original text](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.05196).

Checked: abstract, task/interface, main results, and Appendix D. Under particular English essay-completion conditions, InstructGPT reduced lexical and topic diversity, while base GPT-3 did not show the same significant difference. Do not treat this as a property of all models or experimental validation of the proposed remedies.

## Maintenance policy

This map is a selection as of 2026-10-06, not an exhaustive systematic review. Use the bundled principles for ordinary writing. Recheck when asked for current research, when links fail, or when corrections or generalization across models or media affect a judgment. Prioritize corrections and published versions. Update the inspected scope before strengthening claims about material not previously read.

When adding a source, state which writing decision it changes. More sources alone do not justify more workflow steps. When changing a principle, recheck examples and boundary cases involving content preservation, recognition of the act, sequence, and authorial position. If the design begins invoking dependencies, update the owner's dependency declaration too.
