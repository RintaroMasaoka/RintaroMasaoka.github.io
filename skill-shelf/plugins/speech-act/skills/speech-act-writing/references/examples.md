# Worked examples

All texts, data, policies, and exchanges below are fictional teaching examples. They are not findings from the dossier. The practical diagnoses are design proposals. Ordinary judgments about tense, modality, attribution, and the force of expressions use general linguistic knowledge.

## 1. Drafting a bounded claim from pilot evidence

### Input

Artifact: an abstract sentence. Reader: researchers deciding whether to examine a proposed annotation method. Intended act: report a limited comparison and motivate further study.

Toy evidence: eight passages were annotated under Methods A and B. Six had fewer annotator disagreements under B. The passages were not randomly sampled; no causal design or broad validation was supplied.

Requested wording in the notes:

> Our study proves that Method B solves interpretive ambiguity.

### O1 and O3

The dominant purpose is to present a pilot result. The notes instead attempt a general proof. Changing this is a change in claim and force, not merely a smoother formulation.

Proposed artifact:

> In this eight-passage pilot comparison, annotators disagreed less often on six passages under Method B than under Method A. This pattern motivates a larger comparison across genres.

Consequential changes: the claim is limited to the supplied comparison; the next sentence proposes further investigation. It does not claim that disagreement measures every kind of ambiguity or that the method will generalize. Merely adding probably to the original sentence would leave those problems unresolved.

Desired later effect: readers consider further study. No observation of that effect is supplied. Reader understanding of the bounded claim, belief in its significance, and willingness to use the method remain different questions.

Basis: O1/O3; Austin's act/effect distinction, Grice's attention to communicated information, and the dossier's repeated evidence-scope boundaries. The specific scholarly formulation is an instructional proposal.

## 2. Revising politeness without adding an undertaking

### Input

Artifact: a response to a reviewer. Intended acts: acknowledge a helpful criticism, report one completed clarification, and explain why a requested new experiment is outside the current revision.

Supplied facts: the definition was clarified in the opening paragraph of Section 2. No new experiment was conducted. The author has not undertaken to conduct one.

A model proposes:

> Thank you for the helpful suggestions. We will implement all your recommendations and resolve the issue completely.

### O2

| Passage | Content and force | Conflict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thank you for the helpful suggestions. | Acknowledgment and favorable evaluation | Consistent with the supplied purpose. |
| We will implement all your recommendations | Broad future undertaking | Adds a commitment absent from the brief. |
| resolve the issue completely | Assures a comprehensive result | Neither the supplied work nor evidence supports that assurance. |

### O3

Proposed artifact:

> Thank you for the helpful criticism. We have clarified the opening paragraph of Section 2. A new experiment is outside the current revision, and we have not added one.

This separates acknowledgment, a completed action, and the boundary of the response. If the author wants to undertake later work, that requires a different author choice and different wording. A warm tone does not supply authorization for the undertaking.

The reviewer may understand this response and still disagree. Its politeness does not demonstrate trust, authenticity, acceptance, or ownership. The author may edit the explanation to fit the actual project; the model should not invent a more persuasive reason.

Basis: O2/O3/O4; Austin, Hyland, and the AI dossier's separation of expression, reception, and ownership. Tense and commitment checks are ordinary linguistic applications.

## 3. Repairing an indirect reviewer request

### Input

Reviewer comment:

> The distinction between understanding and agreement could be clearer.

Available context: the comment follows Section 2, where both terms appear. The author has now added a distinction in Section 2's opening paragraph. No reviewer response to this revision is available.

### O2 and O5

Candidate readings:

- A request to revise the definition in the manuscript.
- A request to explain the existing distinction in the response letter.

The comment does not explicitly specify the repair. The manuscript change supports choosing the first reading while making the response explicit.

Proposed artifact:

> We understood this comment as a request to distinguish the two terms in the manuscript. We have revised the opening paragraph of Section 2. Understanding a claim does not by itself mean agreeing with it. Please let us know if your concern was instead a request to explain the existing distinction in this response letter.

| Earlier act | Interpretation used | Response act | Action status | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Indirect criticism/request | Clarify the manuscript distinction | State interpretation, report change, offer repair | Completed clarification | Supplied Section 2 location |

This reply presents a contribution and makes further repair possible. It does not establish that the reviewer understood, agreed, or accepted the revision. If the manuscript had not yet changed, use a future undertaking only if authorized, or describe a proposed revision; do not retain we have revised.

For an article without an active exchange, the corresponding design move might be to define the distinction directly. That would reduce an interpretive dependency, but would not establish joint grounding with unknown readers.

Basis: O2/O5; Grice, Stalnaker, Clark and Brennan, and Bazerman. The proposed clarification is not a tested repair technique.

## 4. Evaluating institutional force and presumed sharing

### Input

Artifact: a submission cover letter written by an author. Intended act: request consideration of a manuscript. Supplied venue rule: only the editor issues acceptance decisions after review. No acceptance decision has occurred.

Draft:

> As we all agree, the controversy is settled. This letter hereby accepts our manuscript for publication.

### O1 and O4

| Finding | Basis | Diagnostic status | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agreement is treated as shared | As we all agree, with no supplied evidence of reader agreement | Underdetermined | State the paper's argument instead of assuming consensus. |
| A settled controversy is asserted | No evidence for that conclusion is supplied | Underdetermined | Bound the claim to the manuscript's actual argument or evidence. |
| The letter attempts an acceptance act | The supplied rule assigns this act to the editor | Conflicts with a supplied condition | Make the author's submission or request explicit. |

Proposed artifact, conditional on the stated intended act:

> We submit this manuscript for consideration.

Two hypothetical reader situations:

- An editor with the supplied rule can recognize a submission request in the revision. Whether the editor accepts it is a separate future outcome.
- A researcher reading the cover letter without the manuscript can recognize the submission request but cannot assess the manuscript from the letter alone.

These are possible readings, not observed responses. The word hereby does not supply institutional authority. If the same wording appeared in an authorized editor's decision under different supplied conditions, its analysis would change.

Basis: O1/O4; Austin's circumstances, Searle's rule distinction, Stalnaker's presumed context, and Bazerman's document/activity relationships. The fictional venue rule is an example input, not a rule inferred from the research.
