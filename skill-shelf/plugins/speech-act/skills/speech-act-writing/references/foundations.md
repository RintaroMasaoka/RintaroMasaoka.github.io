# Foundations and Practical Principles

Reference IDs correspond to the [Source Map](sources.md). The writing methods below are this package's heuristics for integrating several theories, rather than theorems from the original works or a validated universal procedure.

## The Core of Speech Act Theory and This Skill's Extensions

Austin distinguished the locutionary act of stating meaningful content, the illocutionary act of asserting, warning, promising, or doing something else in saying it, and the perlocutionary act of producing effects such as persuasion or surprise by saying it. Successful institutional declarations also require appropriate persons, procedures, circumstances, and other conditions. Uptake of meaning and illocutionary force differs from agreement, emotional response, and execution.[F01]

Searle's analysis separates propositional content from illocutionary force and distinguishes assertions, requests, promises, expressions of thanks, and other acts by their purposes and their direction of fit between words and the world. For example, "propose p as a hypothesis" and "assert that p has been proved" are different acts even though both concern p. A verb classifying the act does not by itself determine what has been done in the actual context.[F02]

Assertions are also speech acts. Do not make propositions and acts addressed to readers mutually exclusive approaches to writing. Design **what to say, what the utterance does, and what is needed for it to reach the reader** together. Focusing only on psychological effects can obscure whether assertions, qualifications, and questions are received as the acts intended.

To address sequence, attention, memory, readers' questions, and roles within an academic community, add pragmatics, rhetoric, genre studies, and cognitive research. This package integrates these into writing practice informed by speech act theory; it does not identify all of them as Austin's or Searle's theory itself.

## What Is Shared and What Is Newly Proposed

Stalnaker's common ground is background information that participants presuppose they share, rather than a set of truths actually known by every reader. Recognizing that an assertion has been made differs from adopting its content as a shared premise.[F04]

**Application to writing:** Distinguish the background readers can reasonably be expected to have, the information the text has already provided, and claims newly proposed for acceptance. Expressions such as "the existing problem," "this effect," "still," and "merely" require shared premises. Do not smuggle a contested evaluation into a presupposition; present it as a claim that readers can judge with its evidence.

Even shared technical vocabulary does not establish that its particular use, conditions, and comparison targets are shared. Providing necessary relationships is not the same as adding explanations for beginners.

## Understanding Sufficient for the Present Purpose

For Clark and Brennan, grounding is the collaborative process of mutually establishing understanding sufficient for the current purpose. The costs of confirmation, rereading, repair, and other activities differ by medium.[F05]

**Application to writing:** Choose the required level of understanding from the task at hand. An overview needs the question and the status of the result; reuse of a calculation needs assumptions and operations; a response to reviewers needs the response to the criticism and the issues that remain. A reader's restatement, an operation applied to an example, or a subsequent action can provide evidence. A simple "I understand" is not necessarily enough.

Because asynchronous papers do not provide immediate responses, anticipate the necessary meaning, conditions, and positioning, and build a structure readers can revisit. This still does not amount to observing actual readers' understanding.

## Do Not Leave Implication Outside the Content Check

Grice's cooperative principle and maxims provide a framework for explaining how utterances are interpreted in relation to a shared purpose and the current stage of an exchange, and how implications beyond literal wording are inferred. They are not commandments that writing must always obey; apparent violations can also generate implicatures.[F03]

**Application to writing:** Check which relationships readers may infer from contrast, examples, sequence, citations, and omission. If A and B are compatible in "A is the case. However, B is the case," check whether a contrast is needed or whether the passage merely changes topics. If placing "B happened after A" implies causation, investigate whether that causal claim is justified. Making only the literal wording safe while strengthening the implication does not resolve insufficient evidence.

The necessary amount of information is determined by what is sufficient to support the present judgment, rather than by sentence length. Removing relevant qualifications can shorten a sentence while encouraging a false generalization.

## What Readers Gain from Their Effort

Wilson and Sperber's relevance theory addresses inference using background and evidence of intentions, and the relationship between cognitive effects and processing effort. It is not a simple numerical ratio or a rule that shorter is better.[F06]

Gopen and Swan explain how structure in English scientific writing supplies cues to the subject of a sentence, connections to what precedes and follows it, and emphasis. They explicitly reject treating their advice as mechanical rules.[F09]

Cowan's research on capacity emphasizes the importance of restricted experimental conditions and the identification of chunks. It does not yield an upper limit on the number of sentences or list items in a text.[F10]

**Application to writing:** Check which question a passage answers and which comparison it makes possible. At the same time, examine how much new notation, distant cross-reference, and unresolved conditional information it asks readers to retain. Difficult content may need to remain; do not add unnecessary reference searching or shifts of topic to that difficulty. Sentence beginnings and headings provide direction, and the body should fulfill that promise. Do not mechanically place all semantic emphasis at sentence endings or in conclusions.

## Genre as Participation in a Community's Work

Miller treats genre as action in recurrent social situations. Swales emphasizes shared communicative purposes and distinguishes the order of conducting research from the order of explaining and justifying it. Bazerman examines how texts connect with other documents and activities to organize people's judgments, relationships, and actions.[F07, F08, F12]

**Application to writing:** Structure a paper as a document through which others can evaluate, place, and reuse results, rather than as a formatted research log. Arrange the question, comparisons, methods, results, and limitations according to the work they perform. Choose moves that match the actual contribution, such as respectfully building on prior work, questioning the conditions under which a conclusion applies, or proposing a new explanation.

Some teaching practices read introductions as rhetorical moves, but do not require every paper to follow "important field -> gap -> we solve it."[F14] Check the expectations of the field, language, and medium, and do not invent a gap that does not exist.

## Recipients as Responding Agents

Bakhtin argues that utterances belong to chains that respond to prior utterances and anticipate future responses. Responses to writing include objections, actions, and understanding that emerges later, as well as agreement.[F11]

Hyland's analysis of academic writing examines how stance and engagement combine according to disciplinary conventions.[F13]

**Application to writing:** Determine which existing claims or questions the text answers and what new questions it enables readers to ask in return. Whether questions or objections need explicit treatment depends on the actual issue. Do not invent a conversation with an imaginary reader in every paragraph. Rather than adding more reader-directed instructions or instances of "we," make it possible to distinguish the evidence for a claim and the limits of that claim.

## When an Act Does Not Succeed

Change the repair according to the cause.

| Observation | Focus of the repair |
|---|---|
| The content is understandable, but its status as a hypothesis or an established result is unclear. | The act's force and positioning, in accordance with the evidence |
| The reader recognizes an assertion but cannot evaluate its reasons. | Evidence, reasoning, and conditions; wording alone cannot supply them |
| The response is polite, but what it answers is unclear. | The target of the criticism and the actual response or action taken |
| There is enough information, but why it appears here is unclear. | The question, comparison, purpose, and sequence |
| A question is posed, but the subsequent text answers another question. | Align the question or subsequent structure with the actual research purpose. |
| The act and reasons are clear, but the reader disagrees. | Consider the objection. Disagreement itself is not a communication failure. |

These distinctions help avoid covering missing evidence with reader address or repairing a structural problem solely by adding more definitions.
